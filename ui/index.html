On-chain guardrails

Five invariants bound every plan the enclave can submit. Each is checked by the vault before execution; a plan that would violate any of them reverts.

◆ Conservation — enforced

What it is. Total assets may not fall across a rebalance. Capital can move between venues; it cannot leave the vault.

Why it exists. Without it, a signed plan could route capital through a venue that paid the attacker on the way through. The conservation check makes routing losses impossible independent of who the venue is.

◇ Venue cap — bounded per venue

What it is. The largest share any single venue is allowed to hold. Shown as the highest cap across active venues.

Why it exists. A compromised or failing venue can only ever lose its own slice; the rest of the vault is unaffected. However confident the strategy is, it cannot concentrate the book past this bound.

↻ Turnover — per rebalance

What it is. The maximum fraction of the book that may be rotated in a single rebalance.

Why it exists. A malicious agent that can act often enough will grind the vault down through cost even without ever failing another check. Turnover + a minimum interval put a ceiling on how fast the book can churn.

± FTSO band — vs FTSO XRP/USD

What it is. The reference price the enclave claims to have used must sit within this band of Flare's FTSO XRP/USD feed.

Why it exists. The claimed price is an input to the agent's decision. Anchoring it to the FTSO feed means an invented price cannot be used to justify an allocation the market did not print.

⌛ Signal freshness — max signal age

What it is. The attested market observation behind a plan must be newer than this threshold, measured from the FDC voting round.

Why it exists. A signal old enough to be stale is a signal old enough to be replayed. The freshness bound plus the monotonic nonce make replay of past decisions impossible.

How it holds together

  1. 01 Private strategy The allocation model runs inside an enclave and never leaves it.
  2. 02 Public inputs The market data it reads is attested on-chain by ~100 providers.
  3. 03 On-chain guardrails Five checks bound every plan before the vault will execute it.
  4. 04 User funds Capital moves between venues. It cannot be moved out.

A malicious enclave can still pick a poor-but-legal allocation, or refuse to act. It cannot move funds out, concentrate, churn, or act on a price the market never printed. The enclave controls strategy quality, never fund safety.

What is Tacit?

Tacit is an ERC-4626 vault over FXRP whose allocation is decided by an autonomous agent running inside a confidential enclave, and whose authority over funds is bounded in Solidity. The agent reads a market signal that ~100 independent Flare Data Connector providers have attested on-chain, decides how to split the book across yield venues, and signs a plan. The vault checks the plan against five invariants and either accepts it or reverts.

A depositor sees an ordinary vault: deposit, withdraw, a share price that rises with yield. Underneath, the strategy is private and the enforcement is public. The design is one sentence: the enclave controls strategy quality, never fund safety.

This is a hackathon submission for Flare Summer Signal, targeting both the Interoperable Asset Products and Confidential Compute Apps bounties. The contribution is the trust boundary between a confidential agent and a public vault — plus an empirical map of what Web2Json can actually do and what happens when a vault is pointed at a real third-party venue instead of a mock.

Why this needs a TEE

The obvious objection is "why not publish the strategy and skip the enclave?" Three answers, in increasing order of importance.

Confidentiality is the product. A published rule is a rule that gets front-run and copied. If you know what a vault will buy before it buys, you can trade ahead of it. If you know its thresholds, you can push an observable metric just past one and force a rebalance, then harvest the turnover you induced. The strategy is the alpha.

Only some things need to be private — and Tacit privatises exactly those. The inputs are public and attested. The resulting allocation is public and on-chain. What stays inside the enclave is the mapping between them: the weights, thresholds, and risk budget. An observer sees what the agent saw and what it did, and still cannot reproduce the rule. That is a much narrower privacy claim than "our vault is a black box."

A TEE alone would not be enough. A confidential agent that also has unbounded authority over funds is a trusted operator with extra steps — you have swapped "trust the manager" for "trust the manager's hardware." Tacit's contribution is the boundary itself: the enclave is given exactly enough authority to be useful and provably no more, so its confidentiality costs the depositor nothing in safety.

How the vault works

The vault holds real FXRP and mints tFXRP shares against it. Standards: ERC-4626, ERC-20, ERC-165, Ownable2Step, Pausable, ReentrancyGuard on every entry point — not only the ones that look risky, because _withdraw calls foreign venue code before shares are burned.

Share-to-asset conversion follows OpenZeppelin's ERC-4626 offset: shares carry a +3 decimal offset over the asset, so a 6-decimal FXRP yields a 9-decimal tFXRP. This makes the classic "first-depositor share-price inflation" attack too expensive to be worth attempting on any realistic vault size.

Two deliberate departures from the OpenZeppelin defaults: pausing stops deposits but never withdrawals (an operator who can freeze exits can rug — the emergency control only blocks new money in and new allocations out), and maxWithdraw is derived from the vault's own liquidity — venues declared delayed exit do not count toward it, so quotes are honest from the first call.

Every entry point is nonReentrant. OpenZeppelin's ERC-4626 is unguarded, which is correct for a vault holding its own assets and wrong for one that calls into foreign code before burning shares. A test named test_venueCannotReenterRedemption builds a venue that re-enters on withdrawal and asserts the guard specifically — having first confirmed by mutation that the test fails when the modifier is removed.

How the autonomous strategy works

Every rebalance runs the same four-step loop.

1 — Observe. The relayer builds a Web2Json attestation request for Coinpaprika's XRP ticker and submits it to FdcHub. Roughly 100 data providers independently fetch the URL, apply the same jq transform, and vote. The round finalises in 90–180 seconds and a Merkle proof is published. Coinpaprika is not an arbitrary choice: the source has to be one the providers will fetch (a smaller set than the verifier accepts), and it is the only attesting source carrying price, volume, and multiple return horizons in a single document.

2 — Decide. The enclave receives the attested observation and per-venue metadata (cap, declared liquidity, realised rate). It computes a risk budget — how much of the vault should be deployed at all — from realised volatility, 24-hour drawdown, and volume. It splits that budget across venues, tilted toward yield. It signs (nonce, deadline, signalHash, refPrice, targets) with its registered identity, binding the signature to this chain and this vault.

3 — Enforce. executeRebalance verifies the FDC proof, checks the plan is bound by hash to that observation, checks the reference price against the FTSO XRP/USD feed, and then re-derives every transfer from its own ledger rather than trusting any number in the plan. The five invariants are asserted against the result.

4 — Settle. Funds move. lastSignal and lastFtsoPriceMicroUsd are stored on-chain so the inputs behind the allocation stay publicly auditable after the fact.

What the enclave does — and does not

The enclave holds the private allocation model, an identity key derived from the TEE, and nothing else. It receives an attested signal, computes a weight vector, and signs (nonce, deadline, signalHash, refPrice, targets). The signature is verified on-chain against teeIdentity(), which is set once at deployment and can only be rotated by the vault owner.

What the enclave cannot do, even if fully compromised:

  • Move funds out of the vault — the ERC-4626 surface is the only exit, and the vault re-derives its own transfers.
  • Breach a venue cap — the resulting allocation is checked venue-by-venue.
  • Exceed the turnover limit — computed on the delta between before and after.
  • Act on a price outside the FTSO band — PriceOutOfBand.
  • Act on an observation the data providers never attested — SignalMismatch.
  • Replay a genuine but stale observation — SignalStale.
  • Replay a plan — monotonic nonce, plus a deadline (PlanExpired).
  • Bill the vault for its own signature — the signature domain is bound to chain id and vault address.

What a malicious enclave can do: pick a legal-but-poor allocation within the bands, or refuse to act. That is opportunity cost, not loss. Deposits remain fully redeemable and the share price cannot fall from it. This is stated plainly rather than buried — a trust model that only lists its strengths is not a trust model.

On-chain guardrails, in depth

Five checks bound every plan before the vault will execute it. Live values are on the Strategy page.

  • Conservation. Total assets may not fall across a rebalance (within a tiny tolerance for integer-division dust). A plan that would shrink the vault is rejected outright, so routing funds through a hostile venue on the way through is impossible independent of who the venue is.
  • Venue cap. No venue may hold more than its configured share. Firelight is registered at 30%; the synchronous venues at 60%. However confident the strategy is, the book cannot be concentrated past these bounds.
  • Turnover. The maximum fraction of the book that may be rotated in one rebalance. This bounds churn, so a malicious enclave cannot bleed the vault through repeated large reshuffles.
  • Minimum interval. Rebalances are spaced by a floor (minRebalanceInterval, currently 300 seconds). Turnover + interval together put a ceiling on how fast the book can churn.
  • FTSO price band. The reference price the enclave claims to have used must sit within a bounded distance (currently ±5%) of Flare's FTSO XRP/USD feed. Decimals are read from the feed rather than hardcoded, because Flare's docs warn they can change.
  • Signal binding. The plan is bound by hash to exactly one attested observation. Compute on any other input and the vault rejects it as SignalMismatch.
  • Signal freshness. The attested observation behind a plan must be newer than maxSignalAge. Freshness is reconstructed from the FDC voting round, not from a timestamp inside the fetched document — a compromised endpoint cannot backdate a round.
  • Monotonic nonce + deadline. Every plan carries a nonce and a deadline; the nonce never regresses and the deadline puts a hard bound on how long a signed plan can wait to be submitted.
  • Chain + vault binding in the signature. The EIP-712 domain includes chain id and vault address, so a plan signed against one deployment cannot be replayed against another.
Depositing (Coston2 direct)

Two signatures: an FXRP approve to the vault, then deposit. Gas is paid in C2FLR on Coston2. The vault mints tFXRP shares directly to the caller.

The UI checks the wallet's FXRP balance before requesting an allowance so the error names the wallet, not the vault — a bare ERC20InsufficientBalance raised inside transferFrom otherwise reads like the vault is broken. If an existing allowance already covers the amount, the approve step is skipped.

Get C2FLR and FXRP from the Coston2 faucet — 100 C2FLR and 10 FXRP per address per 24 hours. FXRP has no public mint on Coston2; the faucet is the only route.

Depositing (Base Sepolia, cross-chain)

One signature on Base Sepolia. The spoke's mint-burn OFT burns the sender's FXRP mirror, LayerZero delivers a compose message to Coston2, the hub adapter releases real FXRP from escrow to the composer, and the composer runs vault.deposit() and forwards the shares. Depositor pays ETH for the LayerZero fee — no Coston2 gas at any point.

Shares terminate on Coston2 by default via a plain transfer — no second LayerZero message, no second fee, no Coston2 native drop. That last part is not stylistic: the Coston2 executor advertises nativeCap = 0 for the Base Sepolia pathway, so a hop that needs native delivered on Coston2 is not fundable at any price. Terminating on the hub sidesteps it entirely.

The inner minAmountLD is floored at previewDeposit(amount) × (1 − 50bps). Not slippage in the AMM sense — an ERC-4626 deposit has no price impact. It absorbs the exchange rate moving between quote and execution: yield accrues, and a rebalance can land in between. 50 bps is generous for a minute-scale window while still catching what matters — the share price moving by orders of magnitude, not fractions of a percent.

Both legs are traceable on LayerZero Scan under the same GUID. Typical end-to-end landing time is under a minute on testnet.

Withdrawing

Hub-only, from Coston2. Withdrawals are served from idle capital first, then by unwinding active venues pro rata rather than in index order. Sequential unwinding concentrates residuals in later venues, which pushes them past shares the enclave itself could never have requested. This bug was found by the invariant fuzzer.

A venue marked delayed exit settles through a queue (Firelight is the live example on Coston2) and does not count toward what you can redeem today. Max quotes what the vault can actually pay right now rather than the position's full value — the vault will not promise funds it cannot pay today.

Every call into a venue — totalAssets, maxWithdraw, withdraw — is wrapped. A venue that reverts, lies, or hangs contributes nothing and everyone else is still served. No single venue can freeze the vault or block redemption.

Cross-chain architecture (LayerZero OVault)

The stack is LayerZero V2 with the Vault Composer pattern:

  • Spoke FXRP OFT — mint-burn OFT on Base Sepolia. Burns the sender's balance directly on send, so no approve is needed.
  • Hub asset adapter — lock-release OFT on Coston2 wrapping real FXRP. Escrow lives on this contract.
  • Hub share adapter — lock-release OFT wrapping tFXRP shares, for the (opt-in) variant where shares bridge back to the spoke.
  • Vault Composer — the LayerZero reference composer, deployed unchanged. Decodes the compose message, calls vault.deposit(), and either transfers the shares locally on the hub or bridges them.

The composer's outbound hop routes through _sendLocal when dstEid == HUB_EID — a plain transfer of the real vault shares, no second LayerZero message, no second fee. This is the default and cheapest route, chosen by setting the hop's dstEid to Coston2's EID in the outer send's composeMsg.

Autopilot & the relayer

The relayer is a one-shot script that runs a full cycle when asked. The autopilot is a small daemon that decides when to run one — so the vault keeps working without every laptop being open.

It acts on two triggers: a RebalanceRequested event (what the UI's Request rebalance button emits) and a heartbeat (default 3600s). Before spending anything it runs a preflight — a cycle costs an FDC fee plus two transactions and takes minutes to fail, so RebalanceTooSoon, a paused vault, a mismatched enclave identity, or an empty relayer wallet are all caught before submission.

Nothing kills it: every exception is logged and followed by exponential backoff (30s → 15min cap). It polls get_logs over explicit block windows with a persisted cursor rather than holding a filter handle — long-lived filters expire silently on public RPCs, and a watcher that goes deaf looks identical to a quiet market.

Neither shifts the trust boundary. The autopilot chooses when a rebalance is attempted, never what it contains. executeRebalance is permissionless and every field in it is attested, signed, or re-derived on-chain. A stopped, stolen, or hostile autopilot is a liveness problem, never a safety one — a stolen autopilot key buys the ability to pay gas and nothing else.

Flare integration, primitive by primitive

FCC (Flare Confidential Compute). Runs the allocation model. The risk budget, volatility bounds, drawdown sensitivity, and yield tilt never leave the enclave. Built as a custom extension with op type TACIT/REBALANCE — deliberately not F_-prefixed and not a reserved command name, because a custom type reusing a reserved command passes local validation and is then silently never delivered.

FDC (Flare Data Connector, Web2Json). Attests Coinpaprika's XRP ticker on-chain — price, 24h volume, and returns over 1h/6h/24h — verified by ~100 independent data providers. Each plan is bound by hash to exactly one attested observation, which must be fresh. Freshness is reconstructed from the FDC voting round as FIRST_VOTING_ROUND_START_TS + votingRound × 90, so a compromised endpoint cannot backdate a timestamp inside its payload.

FTSO. The XRP/USD feed bounds the reference price the enclave claims to have used, to within 5%. This is not a price display — it is the invariant that makes the enclave's output auditable rather than trusted. Without it, refPriceMicroUsd would be an unfalsifiable claim.

FAssets / FXRP. The managed asset, settled as a real ERC-20 on Coston2, resolved from AssetManagerFXRP.fAsset() rather than hardcoded. Six decimals, not 18 — assuming otherwise silently breaks every share price and threshold in the system.

Risk model

What you are trusting, and what you are not.

  • Not trusted: the enclave — bounded by the guardrails above.
  • Not trusted: the relayer or autopilot — every field they carry on-chain is attested, signed, or re-derived on-chain. A stolen autopilot key buys the ability to pay gas and nothing else.
  • Trusted (bounded): the underlying venue contracts — capped at their configured share, isolated per venue, so a failing venue can lose its slice but never freeze the vault or block redemption.
  • Trusted (protocol): Flare's FDC verifier, FTSO, and FXRP asset manager — the same Flare infrastructure any FXRP product depends on.
  • Trusted (LayerZero): LayerZero V2 endpoints, DVNs, and executors on both sides of the cross-chain deposit route — the Coston2 leg additionally depends on the Vault Composer, which is unchanged bytecode from the LayerZero reference.
  • Trusted (operator): the vault owner (Ownable2Step) can pause deposits, register/deactivate venues, update guardrail parameters within Solidity-enforced bounds, and rotate the enclave identity. Cannot move funds. Cannot pause withdrawals.
Failure scenarios

What happens when things go wrong, by component.

  • Enclave dies or refuses to sign. The vault stops rebalancing. Deposits and withdrawals still work. Share price drifts with idle-yield only.
  • Enclave lies within bounds. The strategy underperforms. Share price will not fall from it; the checks make loss-from-plan impossible.
  • FDC round fails or is not attested. The relayer surfaces the failure and does not submit a plan. The vault does not accept unsignalled plans.
  • Autopilot dies. Anyone (including the UI's Request rebalance button) can still initiate a cycle by hand — the daemon only decides when.
  • Venue goes async. The adapter reverts on shares-leaving-without- assets-arriving with AsyncWithdrawalUnsupported; the failing rebalance rolls back rather than booking phantom accounting. Firelight is the live example.
  • Venue reverts, lies, or hangs. Wrapped calls contain it; the other venues and redemption continue serving.
  • FTSO feed becomes stale or reverts. The price band check fails closed — the rebalance reverts with PriceOutOfBand rather than accepting whatever the enclave claims.
  • LayerZero message stalls. The DVN/executor set eventually delivers; if the compose fails on the hub, funds sit at the composer and are recoverable via LayerZero's retry surface.
  • Owner key is compromised. An attacker can pause deposits and misregister venues. They cannot move funds; they cannot stop withdrawals. Recovery is Ownable2Step transfer to a fresh key.

Engineering notes (things that cost real time)

The FDC verifier and the FDC providers are different machines. prepareRequest returning VALID only proves the verifier could reach a URL and run the jq filter. The ~100 data providers that actually attest are different machines with different network egress. A request can be VALID, submitted, paid for on-chain — and then silently never attested. Coinpaprika, Coinbase, and Gemini were confirmed attesting by a controlled experiment; Bitstamp, most other exchanges, and CoinGecko/Binance/Kraken were not.

The FDC request fee lives on a different contract than the hub. FdcHub.getRequestFee reverts — the fee actually lives on FdcRequestFeeConfigurations. The revert text names a method the hub does not expose. Read the fee from the config contract rather than hardcoding.

abi.encodePacked pads array elements to 32 bytes. Standalone value types are not padded, but arrays are. uint16[]{1978, 4770} is 64 bytes, not 4. The Go enclave initially packed two bytes per uint16 (the intuitive reading of "packed") and produced a cryptographically valid signature over the wrong bytes. On-chain it surfaced only as BadSigner, with both sides looking correct in isolation.

The FDC verifier's jq is a subset. floor, round, sub, and ltrimstr are all rejected. Every decimal-to-integer conversion in the signal source is done with string surgery instead of arithmetic. Signed values need startswith("-") branching, because ("-0"|tonumber) silently loses the sign.

OpenZeppelin 5.x defines maxWithdraw in terms of maxRedeem. Overriding both in terms of super sends them into unbounded mutual recursion — a StackOverflow, not a clean revert. Both now derive from a shared non-recursive helper.

Reading the app itself

Vault page. Position, deposit/withdraw, current allocation. Everything you need to manage money in the vault, and nothing you need to understand how it decides.

Strategy page. Live guardrail values, the last attested signal (with its FTSO band check), and the rebalance rhythm. Each guardrail is an expandable card explaining what it is, why it exists, and its current setting.

Activity page. Every Deposit, Withdraw, and Rebalanced event the vault has emitted in the last ~200k blocks, read directly from Coston2. Nothing is off-chain-indexed; every row links to the explorer.

Documentation page. This page.

Testnet balances panel. Both tokens the selected route needs, with a verdict on whether the wallet is funded. Links to the right faucet for the current route. Coston2 gives 100 C2FLR + 10 FXRP per 24h.

Network indicator. Shows the vault chain (Coston2) by default; switches to the wallet's chain once connected. Turns pink when the wallet is on a chain the app does not know about.

Testnet disclaimer

This is a testnet deployment on Flare Coston2 and Base Sepolia. Assets carry no monetary value.

Coston2 does not host the live FXRP money markets (Kinetic, SparkDEX, Enosys), so two of the three venues are testnet stand-ins that pay a modest realised rate; the third uses the generic ERC-4626 adapter to reach a real third-party vault (Firelight stXRP, ~100k FXRP TVL on Coston2). The adapter's behaviour is production-shaped: it flags Firelight as delayed exit, holds back its assets from redemption quotes, and reverts if shares leave without assets arriving.

Hardware attestation of the enclave is simulated on Coston2 — the testnet has no Confidential Space hardware to attest against. Production attestation rejects simulated quotes by design. The five on-chain guardrails do not depend on hardware attestation; turning it on would upgrade the system from bounded to bounded and attested, not close a hole. Deployment is a config change (MODE=0 on a Confidential Space VM plus an allowlisted image hash), not a redesign.

The vault is small — ~2 FXRP, limited by the testnet faucet. The mechanism is what is being demonstrated, not the size of the position.
